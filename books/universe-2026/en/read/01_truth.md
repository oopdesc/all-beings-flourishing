# Chapter 1. What Are We Looking for When We Seek Truth?

Wanting to know the universe’s essence contains several different desires. We want to know how the world works, why such a world exists, and what we should value within it. Each matters deeply, but one answer need not satisfy all three.

Making this distinction first is not an attempt to fragment the questions. It helps us avoid mistaking an explanation of something else for the answer we sought.

<a id="h-2"></a>

## Description, Reasons, and Values

Tilt a glass and water spills. Calculating its flow describes what happens. Asking why the governing law holds seeks a more basic explanation. Deciding who should clean up the water or how to protect someone from slipping raises questions about action and value.

The distinction remains when our subject becomes the whole world. Finding a model that explains cosmic expansion well does not, by itself, determine why the universe exists or who ought to be happy. Conversely, a philosophy that values happiness cannot by itself predict the expansion rate.

The same word can conceal the boundary. Does a “meaningful universe” mean a universe with an explainable structure, one intended by someone, or one in which we can find value? Clarifying the intended meaning changes the evidence required.

<a id="h-3"></a>

## Is Observation a Copy of the World?

A telescope does not deliver an answer containing the universe in its entirety. It delivers signals recorded by a detector, shaped by the instrument’s properties. Through calibration and analysis, we estimate brightness, position, distance, and other quantities.

Theory enters this process, but that does not make every observation a fabrication. Looking through glasses differs from seeing nothing. What matters is investigating the glasses and checking the same object through different ones.

A conclusion has more reasons to withstand scrutiny when methods with different sources of error also support it. Yet two papers using the same data need not amount to two fully independent confirmations. This caution will recur in both cosmology and AI research.

<a id="h-4"></a>

## Two Ideas That Explain the Same Results

Consider a simple calculation. Suppose the observed values are 0, 1, and 2 when x is 0, 1, and 2. One rule says that y equals x. Another adds to x the product of x, x minus 1, and x minus 2.

In symbols, the rules are y = x and y = x + x(x − 1)(x − 2). They agree at all three observed points. At x = 3, however, the first gives 3 and the second 9. Agreement with three points cannot distinguish them.

This is a mathematical example constructed for explanation, not actual cosmological data. It illustrates the difference between fitting past results and correctly predicting results not yet seen. Complexity does not make any explanation acceptable, nor does brevity guarantee truth. Predictions under new conditions open a route to comparison.

<a id="h-5"></a>

## Before Reducing Everything to One Essence

“Everything is information,” “everything is consciousness,” and “everything is vibration” can feel unifying. But if the word merely applies to stones, pain, and stars alike, it has not yet acquired the power to distinguish what will happen.

For example, “everything is information” requires specifying what counts as information, which states are distinguished, and in which physical system it is realized. Information measured through code length does not mean the same thing as news that matters to someone.

I do not want to abandon the hope of unified understanding. Before gathering everything into one word, however, I want to check whether apparently separate explanations really connect. Relating water temperature to molecular motion requires saying what is averaged, what is discarded, and where the account applies. That is stronger than a resemblance between words.

<a id="h-6"></a>

## What Is Lost When We Make Questions Smaller?

Might reducing inquiry to small, measurable questions drain life of its urgency? There is reason for that concern. What is easy to measure is not necessarily what matters. Reducing pain to one number can omit the circumstances that make it painful.

Rather than discard questions we cannot measure, we can continue two tasks: preserve the large question and develop concrete comparisons within it. We may ask whether the universe has a purpose while studying how it has changed. We respect the scope of the resulting answer, then return to what remains.

This movement is the form of inquiry I hope to pursue here. We need neither wait to understand everything before beginning nor mistake a small understanding for the whole answer. We can improve our questions as understanding grows. Let us begin by reading the universe’s light.
