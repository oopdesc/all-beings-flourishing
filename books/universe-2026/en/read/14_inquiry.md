# Chapter 14. Developing Questions We Can Check

Might the universe be interconnected? Where does consciousness arise? Why do people fail to cooperate, and what would change that? Large questions can give us reasons to begin even before their outlines are clear.

Bringing them closer to science requires repeated reformulation. What result would support an idea? What would make us reconsider? Would another explanation produce the same result? Narrowing a question need not abandon the original wish; it finds somewhere to investigate.

<a id="h-2"></a>

## Unpacking a Claim into Three Layers

Consider “consciousness arises from integrating information.” As stated, the kind of information, integration, and consciousness remain open.

The first layer defines meaning: objects, conditions, and the scope of terms. Does consciousness mean reportability or subjective experience? Those meanings cannot be exchanged opportunistically after an experiment.

The second layer is inference: which observable differences follow from definitions and assumptions? Rather than collecting examples called integration, ask what should change when integration differs.

The third is measurement: which instruments, tasks, and analyses investigate those differences? When an indicator is a proxy rather than the target, its correspondence also needs testing.

Philosophy enriches the first layer; mathematics tightens the second; science revisits both through the third. Actual research moves back and forth rather than along a neat one-way pipeline. Separating the layers still helps locate the present difficulty.

<a id="h-3"></a>

## Five Questions for Finding Assumptions

First, does a word change meaning midway? Watch for “information” moving without explanation between communicative distinctions, physical states, and subjective meaning.

Second, what model lies between what we observe and what we want to know? Assumptions support moves from telescope light to mass, brain activity to experience, and text to intelligence.

Third, can another explanation yield the same result? Do not treat failure to find one as victory. Try constructing a close alternative yourself, then seek predictions that still differ.

Fourth, is the information independent? Do not count reanalyses of one dataset, measurements sharing calibration, or responses from one research group as many independent confirmations.

Fifth, what was reselected after seeing results? Records of exploration can acknowledge selection and support subsequent testing.

These questions are not only for rejecting others’ claims. They become a foundation when directed at our own cherished ideas.

<a id="h-4"></a>

## Setting Research Priorities

I do not prioritize papers by date alone. First choose questions most likely to alter the book’s conclusions. Then consult primary sources presenting the relevant data and methods, and seek sources treating the same question under different assumptions. Older foundational definitions and theorems remain necessary.

For dark energy, for instance, compare new observations with updated supernova calibration, alternative parameterizations, and different model-comparison measures. This helps distinguish changes due to observations from changes in how the question is posed.

What matters is which uncertainties were reduced, not merely paper counts. Important work may remain outside the selected scope. Record search dates, issues searched, and passages read so others can continue. This book is not an exhaustive review of all world literature.

<a id="h-5"></a>

## A Small Template for Records

For each claim, keeping these six items together makes revision easier.

1. What is being claimed?
2. What was observed or calculated?
3. Which assumptions does the conclusion require?
4. What are the competing explanations and shared data?
5. What result would change the conclusion?
6. Which version and passages were actually consulted?

Apply the same template to your own ideas. Records fail if famous papers face scrutiny while personal wishes receive exemptions.

<a id="h-6"></a>

## Connecting New Ideas to Existing Ones

Could relationships or structures survive changes in theories? Can multiple research perspectives and practices still yield knowledge of reality? Philosophy of science has developed these questions through structural realism and approaches emphasizing perspectives.<a id="cite-45"></a>[[45]](19_references.md#ref-45)<a id="cite-46"></a>[[46]](19_references.md#ref-46)

My approach of connecting explanations across scales touches that history. I do not claim to unify those schools or resolve their problems. Knowing earlier questions prevents mistaking an idea for an unprecedented discovery and lets us learn existing difficulties.

Prior work does not make further thinking pointless. Which language makes a connection understandable? Which example permits checking? Which purposes should it serve? Readers and writers still have work to continue.

<a id="h-7"></a>

## What Remains When an Error Is Found

Finding a cherished idea wrong does not erase the time spent on it. It identifies a blocked route and narrows the next question. Turning that into learning requires recording what was wrong.

Keeping only revised conclusions makes repeated errors easier. Briefly retain the old claim, the reason for change, and the uncertainty that remains. This makes inquiry a transferable undertaking rather than only an exercise in assigning blame.

Pursuing truth may not mean always standing on the correct side. It means moving toward forms in which our own possible errors can be found concretely. The next two chapters use mathematics and science closer to hand for that purpose.
