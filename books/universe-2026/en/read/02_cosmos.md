# Chapter 2. Reading Cosmic History in Light

We can learn about the universe’s past because light takes time to arrive. Looking at a distant object means receiving a message from its past. Yet “farther means older” does not, on its own, let us read cosmic history directly. The universe itself has changed in size while that light traveled.

<a id="h-2"></a>

## What Stretched Wavelengths Tell Us

Elements emit and absorb light at characteristic wavelengths. When the pattern from a distant object is shifted overall toward longer wavelengths, we can measure that shift. In the standard description of large-scale cosmic expansion, this redshift is related to the change in the scale of space between emission and the present.

Redshift alone does not uniquely determine distance or age. An object’s own motion and gravitational effects also contribute, and estimating very large distances requires a model of expansion history. Separating the observed wavelength ratio from model-derived distance helps avoid confusion later.

This also matters for the picture of stars all fleeing from us. Cosmic expansion is not simply an explosion at the center of preexisting space, throwing matter into an outer void. It describes a change in the scale of space used to express distances between objects on large scales.

<a id="h-3"></a>

## Light Remaining from the Early Universe

The cosmic microwave background is an important clue to the early universe. It brings information from after light became able to travel freely over great distances, following an earlier hot period of strong interaction between matter and light. It is almost uniform across the sky but contains small variations, related to early fluctuations and to the universe’s subsequent composition.

Planck’s cosmological parameter analysis described the microwave background and other data precisely within a flat ΛCDM model with six base parameters. In that reference model, the estimated age of the universe is about 13.8 billion years. This is not a clock read continuously since the beginning; it is an estimate obtained by comparing observations with a model.<a id="cite-1"></a>[[1]](19_references.md#ref-1)

That does not make the age estimate unimportant. Stating its assumptions gives the number a more precise meaning rather than weakening it. If the model later needs revision, we can identify what should change.

<a id="h-4"></a>

## Inferring Accelerated Expansion

Research using distant supernovae in 1998 was an important starting point for understanding that cosmic expansion is accelerating. Observed brightness and redshift were compared with models of expansion. This was not a direct observation of dark energy’s identity.<a id="cite-2"></a>[[2]](19_references.md#ref-2)

Here the strengths and difficulties of cosmology come together. We cannot recreate the entire universe in a laboratory. We can nevertheless investigate objects at different distances and epochs, asking whether multiple observations support the same history. We must also examine conditions beyond the instrument itself: how brightness is treated, dust, and how samples are selected.

The actual work of science lies between “it depends on theory, so it cannot be trusted” and “the number is precise, so it is settled.” It involves looking for kinds of error, not merely reducing error bars.

<a id="h-5"></a>

## What We Can See and What Exists

The “observable universe” concerns the region from which information can reach us. It does not mean that we have found a wall marking the boundary of everything that exists. Nor does it mean every speculation about what lies outside our present view is equally well established.

The sea visible from shore need not end where our sight ends. But this metaphor cannot tell us which islands lie beyond it. For the universe, there may be fundamental limits on obtaining information at all, demanding even greater care than the seaside comparison suggests.

In seeking the universe’s essence, I do not want to turn invisibility into a license for certainty. Models of unobservable regions may still be compared if they have consequences in observable regions. Without such consequences, they cannot be checked by the same methods, at least.

<a id="h-6"></a>

## When Components Remain Unidentified

In ΛCDM, Λ denotes the cosmological constant and CDM cold dark matter. The name might sound like a finished inventory of substances whose identities are known. Yet successfully playing a role in explaining observations differs from knowing the entity or mechanism responsible.

This is not simply a story of cosmology failing. A structure whose predictions fit well can contain ingredients still unidentified. The next two chapters follow research carrying both facts together. Rather than choose between believing and doubting the invisible, we will ask what is unseen and what can nevertheless be measured.
