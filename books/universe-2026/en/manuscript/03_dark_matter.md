# Chapter 3. How Can We Test for Invisible Matter?

Dark matter is not permission to introduce any invisible thing we like. The question is how far a single explanation can account for observed motion, the bending of light, and other phenomena. A central clue has been the mismatch between mass distributions inferred through gravity and the distribution of luminous matter.

## Explaining Several Observations Together

Studying a galaxy’s rotation and studying the bending of light around a galaxy cluster do not measure the same quantity with the same instrument. Constraints from the microwave background and large-scale structure add further requirements. A good explanation must address these different conditions, not just one striking phenomenon.

For the colliding clusters known as the Bullet Cluster, Clowe and colleagues reported an offset between hot gas traced by X-rays and the mass distribution inferred through gravitational lensing. This is strong evidence that the position of gas containing much of the ordinary matter cannot by itself explain the distribution of gravity. It does not, however, identify a particular dark matter particle.[3]

Distinguishing evidence for something from a complete explanation of that thing clarifies where research stands. Multiple lines of support for an unseen mass component can coexist with uncertainty about its particle identity and its interactions with ordinary matter. Those require further observations.

## Waiting Underground

If candidate dark matter particles occasionally interact with ordinary matter, we might detect their traces on Earth. Experiments therefore suppress backgrounds such as cosmic rays and use well-characterized detectors to search for candidate reactions.

A null result also carries information. It does not rule out all dark matter. For assumed combinations of particle mass and interaction strength, it narrows the region where the experiment should have seen a signal. Knowing where we have searched changes where we should look next.

Conversely, a single rare event does not establish discovery. Backgrounds from ordinary physical processes remain: the instrument, radioactive material, accidental coincidences, and more. In a search for something new, understanding the background is as important as the possible discovery itself.

## One Event Reported in 2026

In September 2026, the LZ collaboration reported a search extending to higher recoil energies. An analysis with 2.84 tonne-years of exposure contained one event consistent with a nuclear recoil of approximately 248 keV. The paper gives a global significance of 2.6σ after accounting for the breadth of the search. This must not be confused with the maximum local significance of 3.4σ.[4]

This is not a confirmed discovery of dark matter. The paper considers rare background possibilities. The event occurred in data collected in 2023, whereas the analysis was published in 2026. A report’s publication date and an event’s date must be read separately.[4]

What can we take from this? Searching a previously less explored range can produce new events to investigate. Yet after the excitement of seeing something rare, the task of distinguishing causes remains. Novelty is a reason to continue research, not a substitute for evidence that settles an answer.

## Significance Is Not the Probability of an Identity

We cannot translate 2.6σ into “more than a 99 percent probability that this is dark matter.” The test assesses how unusual the data are under a specified background model. It does not directly measure the probability of the dark matter hypothesis after comparing every candidate explanation.

If the background model misses a process, its assessment of rarity can change. Searching many locations or candidates also increases the opportunities to find something unusual somewhere. We therefore need an assessment covering the search range and subsequent independent confirmation. [Chapter 12](12_evidence.xhtml) explores this interpretation through a fictional numerical example.

## Two Routes Toward Understanding

There are at least two routes in dark matter research: explaining gravitational behavior in astronomical systems and the universe as a whole, and detecting candidate-particle reactions experimentally and measuring their properties. Future work must compare whether answers from these routes refer to the same thing.

Modifying gravity should not be rejected merely as unconventional. But such proposals must explain the many phenomena covered by existing accounts. Resolving one dissatisfaction does not complete the comparison, and this chapter cannot assess every alternative.

We need not rush toward either “it is invisible, so anything goes” or “it is invisible, so it does not exist.” If something unseen exists, what traces should its relation to visible things leave? This question will also help when we turn to consciousness. Its usefulness in both contexts does not imply that dark matter and consciousness share a mechanism.
