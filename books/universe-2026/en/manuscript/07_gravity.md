# Chapter 7. At the Boundary Between Gravity and Quantum Theory

General relativity and quantum theory each explain many phenomena. Deep questions remain about where and how to use them together. Two great theories do not require us to declare both wrong. We need to know where they apply and where their explanations must be reconnected.

## Investigating Strong Gravity

When black holes merge, changes in spacetime propagate as gravitational waves. Signals allow estimates of mass and spin before and after merger, which can be compared with relations predicted by general relativity. This opens a route to strong gravity different from telescope images.

Research on GW250114 uses an unusually clear signal to examine area changes during merger and consistency with rotating-black-hole descriptions.[18] Another analysis studies components of the decaying post-merger oscillations to test Kerr black hole predictions.[19] These address different questions using the same event; they are not observations of many independent mergers.

They support predictions of general relativity within the tested range. They neither reveal the inside of a singularity nor select gravity’s final theory. Searching for theoretical limits can also confirm more precisely where a theory works well.

## Area Laws and Evaporation Are Different

Testing a black hole area law is not the same as observing Hawking radiation. The former compares horizon-area relations during merger with quantities inferred from gravitational waves and related measurements. The latter concerns radiation predicted through reasoning involving quantum fields.

Even when both are associated with a famous researcher’s name, we must follow which quantity was checked and by which method. Otherwise, an important observation can be presented as establishing a different phenomenon that remains unseen.

In pursuing the universe’s essence, small shifts of wording matter. Large errors arise not only from baseless sentences but also from extending sound research slightly beyond its meaning.

## Proposals to Superpose Small Masses

In 2017, Bose and colleagues and Marletto and Vedral proposed approaching gravity’s quantum character by allowing masses in quantum superpositions of position to interact gravitationally.[15][16] The question is what we could learn if gravity generated entanglement between two systems.

The appeal is to approach a fundamental question through small, controlled laboratory systems rather than only immense accelerators or the earliest universe. A proposal, however, is not an achieved experiment with every necessary control. Nongravitational interactions and environmental effects must also be distinguished.

Even if such entanglement were observed, which theories would it exclude? Research checking the premises of the argument becomes crucial here.

## Assumptions Reexamined in October 2026

Di Biagio’s 2026 paper examines whether detecting gravitationally induced entanglement alone can certify a nonclassical gravitational field in a theory-independent way. One issue is that information-theoretic locality in a theorem must not be identified automatically with causal constraints between separated spacetime locations.[17]

The paper argues that accounts involving classical gravitational fields remain relevant, making quantitative comparison of predictions important. The experiment is not rendered meaningless; the conclusions it licenses are narrowed by their premises. The journal publication date is October 19, 2026.[17]

Part of the background is Aziz and Howl’s 2025 theory, which treats quantum fields on classical spacetime and proposes an entangling mechanism.[44] That does not immediately invalidate earlier theorems. We must ask whether this model satisfies their assumptions about state spaces, mediation, and locality.

The model itself also faces criticism. A 2026 preprint by Xue and colleagues examines finite wave packets and kinetic energy, questioning the proposed effect’s interpretation and size.[48] Marletto and colleagues’ response asks whether entanglement arises at all and, if it does, what mediates it.[49] These are theoretical criticisms and responses, not results of a new experiment.

We should therefore distinguish checking logical conditions for certification from checking whether a particular classical model is physically viable. Reading both takes us beyond a simple yes-or-no debate. What signal is predicted under achievable conditions, and how can it be separated from other interactions and noise? Such comparison makes the next experiment concrete.

Pausing between the slogans “entanglement means gravity is quantum” and “gravity need not be quantum” reveals the real issues. Which interactions are allowed? What counts as classical? Which locality is assumed? Where do measured predictions differ? The experiment’s value lies in these concrete comparisons.

## Examining the Seams of Explanations

Here I see a method worth carrying into our inquiry. Before declaring a new theory, examine the seams of existing arguments. Do premise words change meaning midway? Is a conclusion about one class of models extended to all models? Is a detected quantity identified with the thing we want to understand?

This book has not newly discovered that method of physics. The studies themselves perform such checks. We can learn to carry their work concretely into other questions.

The universe’s essence need not hide only behind a dramatic sentence. A next question may lie where two good explanations do not quite meet. Finding that difference requires reading both carefully.
