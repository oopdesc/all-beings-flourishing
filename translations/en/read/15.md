# Chapter 15 Examining Cooperation Through a Small Model

<a id="s2"></a>

## Why Use Numbers and Where They Stop

We now make some philosophical assumptions explicit. This is not a discovery of a new social law. It illustrates a public-goods structure familiar in cooperation research through a small fictional example.[43]

These numbers measure neither actual happiness nor reproduced experimental data. They are hypothetical scores for tracking a calculation. Changed premises change results.

Three people undertake a joint activity. Each chooses whether to contribute one resource unit. Contributions double in value and are shared equally among all three. Payoff here means one's share minus one's own contribution.

<a id="s3"></a>

## Everyone Benefits from Contributing Yet May Not

If all contribute one, three doubles to six and each receives two. Subtracting one's contribution leaves a payoff of one each.

If none contributes, nothing is distributed and each payoff is zero. Comparing these outcomes, universal contribution is better for everyone.

Individual choice differs. Contributing one adds only two divided by three, or two-thirds, to one's own share, at a cost of one. One's payoff falls by one-third.

Holding others' choices fixed, noncontribution always yields one-third more. If all three maximize only these individual payoffs, their choices lead to no contribution. The better result for everyone does not follow from separate maximization.

<a id="s4"></a>

## What the Example Shows

Better calculation alone need not produce cooperation. Accurate calculation of private payoff leads to noncooperation here. The problem is the incentive structure, not a computational mistake.

Conditions can change: future relationships, reliable rewards for contributions, or a joint arrangement under agreed conditions. Each requires investigation.

“More information solves it” also needs caution. Complete knowledge of this mechanism leaves the payoff structure unchanged. Distinguish real cases where correcting expectations matters from conflicts of interest information alone cannot solve.

<a id="s5"></a>

## What It Does Not Show

Actual people do not act only on these stipulated payoffs. They may value fairness, others, trust, and norms. The 2026 international experiment in Chapter 8 reported many choosing cooperation under particular conditions.[37]

That experiment and this fictional three-person model have different designs. Neither can be used directly to declare the other's result or calculation wrong, because premises and objects differ.

This difference starts research connecting models with reality: what influences choices, and what changes under different institutions? Derive predictions and revise assumptions through observations. Without that exchange, equations alone do not explain society.
