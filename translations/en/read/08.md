# Chapter 8 Creating Conditions for Cooperation

<a id="s2"></a>

## Goodwill Alone Cannot Sustain Cooperation

People share tasks they cannot solve alone: researching illness, maintaining roads, accumulating knowledge, or responding to disasters. Parts of our lives presuppose cooperation.

Cooperation benefiting the whole differs from each person having a reason to cooperate. If one can enjoy an outcome without contributing, noncontribution may pay. Where contributors alone become exhausted while everyone benefits equally, goodwill bears disproportionate burdens.

Nowak's account of cooperation in evolution examines several mechanisms, including kinship, reciprocity, and interaction structure. It treats cooperation as a phenomenon with conditions rather than something arising unconditionally.[4]

Andre and colleagues' June 2026 study reported that 69% cooperated in an incentivized two-person experiment involving 101,123 people across 125 countries, with expectations of others' cooperation tending to be too low.[37] When both participants chose to forgo part of their private payment, a climate donation was made. These were choices under those payoffs, recipients, and instructions. They do not show that real conflicts or long-term joint projects resolve at the same rate, or that everyone shares one value system.

This research does not directly determine one institutional design for human society. Nor is what evolves necessarily ethically desirable. Still, investigating conditions supporting cooperation matters beyond merely urging everyone to cooperate.

<a id="s3"></a>

## What Shared Purposes Leave Unresolved

Organizations sharing a stated purpose still disagree over interpretation, costs, benefits, and urgency. Agreement does not equal identical tasks or available time.

Ten people may want a community study group, yet if one alone books rooms, prepares materials, and cleans up, it will become unsustainable. Stating the same purpose does not replace fair burdens.

Make tasks visible, clarify capacity, rotate roles, and reduce the activity if needed. Merely demanding endurance because the purpose matters makes joint activity depend on one person's sacrifice.

Reasons for nonparticipation differ. Someone seeking benefits without costs differs from someone lacking time or resources, information, or trust after past harm. Without examining reasons, institutions may exclude the vulnerable first.

<a id="s4"></a>

## No Institutional Panacea

Ostrom criticized attempts to solve environmental and resource management everywhere with one institution, offering a framework to diagnose institutions and conditions in context.[5] A single contrast—common or private property, state or market—cannot capture actual differences.

This book likewise rejects both abolishing capitalism as a solution to everything and assuming markets always improve matters. Price coordination can help in some situations; public services and collective management matter in others. Investigate whose interests a mechanism protects and whose burdens it creates.

Competition is not identical to conflict in every case. With fair rules, voluntary participation, and protected livelihoods for losers, it may develop skills and ideas. Competition over status or survival can also produce concealment and exhaustion. Conditions and effects matter more than the label alone.

Nor does every economic activity require one person's gain to equal another's loss. New value can be created. Who receives it and who bears its costs remain separate questions.

<a id="s5"></a>

## Questions for Designing Cooperation

Clarify what will be done together and what remains individual. Make information and work visible; explain burdens and benefits. Establish decision procedures and routes for help.

Set a time to examine outcomes. Satisfied participants may coexist with harms outside the group. Check effects on nonparticipants and those unable to speak easily. Investigate failures and preserve options to reduce or stop the activity.

This design need not arise simply from mistrust. Well-intentioned people have limits; organizations dependent only on goodwill collapse when those people become exhausted. Sustaining cooperation requires protecting cooperators.

Shared purposes orient institutions; institutions turn purposes into daily action. Neither alone suffices. Later mathematical models revisit this relationship.
