# Chapter 20 What Mathematics Does and Does Not Guarantee

<a id="s2"></a>

## Meaning Before More Symbols

Mathematics clarifies assumptions and relationships. It can make consequences of changes and incompatible conditions easier to follow than words alone.

Naming variables consciousness, happiness, or cosmic purpose does not make them measurable. Without correspondence to reality, value assignment, units, predictions, and error, calculations remain within symbols.

An attractive curve from random data is not evidence about real consciousness or well-being. Simulation examines consequences of assumptions; whether those assumptions fit the world requires separate checks.

<a id="s3"></a>

## Contradiction Is Not the Same as an Open Question

An unknown answer differs from logically incompatible conditions. More information may help the former; changing conditions or definitions is necessary for the latter.

Requiring something and its negation in the same sense, time, and conditions cannot be satisfied in ordinary classical logic. Claiming greater intelligence can always satisfy both requires a different logic or interpretation.

Apparent contradictions may instead involve different meanings or times, resolved by clarification. A broad statement that everything contains contradictions should not obscure particular differences.

<a id="s4"></a>

## What Incompleteness Theorems Establish

The first incompleteness theorem, originating with Gödel and strengthened by Rosser, establishes undecidable sentences in consistent, effectively axiomatized formal systems capable of sufficient arithmetic.[14][47] These conditions matter.

It does not directly establish that nothing in the universe is complete, nothingness cannot exist, or humans necessarily surpass machines. Transferring a theorem requires showing that its conditions apply and explaining the conclusion's meaning.

Epistemic humility matters without stretching a theorem to support it. Strong conclusions under precise conditions are an important lesson from mathematics.

<a id="s5"></a>

## Formal Proof and Correctness About the World

Formal verification checks whether conclusions follow from chosen definitions and axioms, reducing human oversight. Adequate representation of lives and justified ethical premises require examination beyond formalization too.

A program might be proved to maximize a sum of well-being scores correctly. If measurement is unsuitable, it correctly maximizes the wrong object. Correct implementation and a correct purpose must be distinguished.

This book offers arguments under limited assumptions and small reproducible calculations, not a mechanically verified proof of universal ethics. Clarify what can be formalized and what judgments remain outside it.

<a id="s6"></a>

## Respecting the Unknown

Admitting ignorance can seem to silence us. Yet identifying its scope narrows the next investigation: definitions, measurement methods, or comparisons of explanations.

I want to preserve the scale of our questions without stacking unverified calculations or prestigious names beneath them. Saying something small but well grounded about what we learned supplies a foothold for further inquiry.
