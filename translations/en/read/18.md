# Chapter 18 Studying Consciousness and Thinking About Subjectivity

<a id="s2"></a>

## Distinguishing What Needs Explanation

Consciousness can mean wakefulness, reportable information, reflection on one's actions, or feeling something. Changing meanings midargument makes explaining one appear to explain all.

This book particularly concerns experience. A function processing pain and pain feeling distressing are at least distinguishable questions. Studying related functions matters, but its object must be clear.

Reporting experience also involves memory, attention, and language. Brain activity accompanying reports may support experience itself or the act of reporting. Research designs attempt to reduce that confusion.

<a id="s3"></a>

## Comparing Competing Explanations

The 2025 Cogitate study compared predictions of integrated information theory and global neuronal workspace theory using prespecified methods. Results supported some predictions and challenged others in both; no single theory resolved consciousness.[8]

Theoretical responses also question what was tested.[35] Read results, interpretations, and proposed theoretical revisions separately.

Science advances here by specifying predictions and comparing explanations under common conditions rather than adding plausible stories to a broad word. Unsupported predictions should inform further theory and experiments, not be hidden.

A limited perception experiment nevertheless does not test every aspect of consciousness at once. The breadth of conclusions must match the object studied.

<a id="s4"></a>

## Searching for the Self

Introspection raises the question of where “I” am. Something seems to observe sensation, memory, and thought; trying to observe that observer seems repeatedly to exchange observer and observed.

This can question a fixed self. Not finding it as an internal object does not immediately establish that no subject exists or that all are one. The search method's suitability also matters.

Conversation's meaning cannot be located at one point on a page, yet meaning may operate. Similarly, looking for the self as an object may not answer every question about it. This is an analogy for examining questions, not proof of the self's nature.

Introspection importantly indicates how experience appears to us. Explaining its mechanisms or identity with others' experience also requires information beyond introspection.

<a id="s5"></a>

## Do Not Leap Through Physics Vocabulary

Brains and bodies belong to the physical world, so physics matters. Merely adding “quantum,” “information,” “resonance,” or “emergence” does not explain experience.

If invoking emergence, specify what emerges from which components under which conditions. Even if information changes capability, altered capability does not itself establish the arising of experience.

Nor should physical debates about observation and measurement be converted into claims that wishes freely create reality. Interactions with measuring apparatus and subjective desires require different explanations.

<a id="s6"></a>

## Proceeding with Mysteries Unresolved

Incomplete explanation leaves research possible: examine experience reports and bodily states, compare predictions, expand evidence for nonhuman experience, and examine assumptions behind artificial indicators.

Philosophy also asks what explanation should tell us, what counts as the same experience, and how far third-person description replaces subjectivity. Neither science nor philosophy need dismiss the other for what remains missing.

We can care for someone reporting suffering without a final consciousness theory. Their lives need not wait for theoretical completion. I want inquiry into the unknown and an ethical response to known urgency together.
