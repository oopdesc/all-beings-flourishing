# Chapter 14 Self-Transcendence as the Capacity for Revision

<a id="s2"></a>

## What We Transcend

By self-transcendence I mean not making yesterday's understanding the limit of today's. It is the capacity to encounter new information, hear others' experience, and revise our framework.

Learning someone's circumstances may change our belief that they lack effort. Discovering hidden harm may alter our use of a convenient technology. Recognizing a counterexample may make us rewrite a cherished theory. These are concrete revisions, not transformations into a supernatural being.

What we transcend is not another's worth or dignity. Turning it into competition to rise above weaker people departs from expanding concern. I do not want the word to place others beneath us.

<a id="s3"></a>

## Protecting Freedom Not to Grow

Valuing revision creates no duty of constant growth. We also need time to enjoy learning, live steadily without change, rest, and recover.

“You can go higher” may encourage or pressure someone by denying their present life. Their condition and freedom to choose make the difference.

Everyone need not develop at the same speed for everyone's happiness to matter. One person may advance while another rests. People receiving support are not society's backwardness. Institutions for revision should include room not to hurry.

<a id="s4"></a>

## Purposes Too Must Be Revisable

Revising means while treating purposes as eternally correct can efficiently achieve a wrong purpose. Ask why capacities are being developed.

This book's common purpose is no sacred, fixed slogan. Does “all beings” include enough? Does “happiness” omit experiences? Does concern take away freedom? Real objections must be able to change it.

Revising purposes nevertheless differs from abandoning promises whenever inconvenient. Explain reasons, affected people, and responsibilities. Combining freedom to admit mistakes with responsibility to explain makes revision trustworthy.

<a id="s5"></a>

## Is the Whole World Transcending Itself

Evolution, cultural change, and scientific progress can make the world seem to transcend itself. As philosophical imagination, that view has appeal.

Particular changes do not entail one cosmic will or a law that everything necessarily advances toward happiness. Change includes collapse and loss. Scientific evolution does not mean ethically good progress.

This book therefore does not adopt repeated cosmic self-transcendence as an observationally established explanation. For people and societies, specify revision and investigate effects. For the whole universe, ask which observations distinguish it from alternatives.

<a id="s6"></a>

## No Final Form Does Not Mean No Improvement

Some improvements are recognizable without knowing a final state: someone gains library access, hazardous work decreases, misinformation is corrected. Agreement about particular changes can exist without complete agreement on every value.

But gains can entail losses: convenience against privacy, income against family time. Rather than conceal these under “progress,” specify both.

Self-transcendence is no prophecy of inevitable completion. It is a policy of leaving routes for reconsideration open even when we think we have finished. I want it in ways of living, writing books, and designing institutions.
