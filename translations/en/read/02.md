# Chapter 2 Connecting Facts and Values

<a id="s2"></a>

## Different Tasks Within One Sentence

“People cannot live without others' cooperation. Therefore, we should value everyone.” This reads naturally, but its halves perform different tasks. The first describes human life; the second proposes how to act.

Interdependence alone does not necessarily entail a duty of mutual care. Someone could infer instead that exploiting others is advantageous. Moving from the first half to the second requires value premises: respect others' lives and consider harm as well as benefits.

Hiding those premises makes science appear to dictate a single ethics. Stating them lets readers identify agreement and doubt. That is more useful for continuing discussion.

Nor does this book make facts and values unrelated. Even after choosing to reduce suffering, observation and research are needed to identify effective assistance. Values direct investigation; facts revise our means and expectations. We can connect them without confusing them.

<a id="s3"></a>

## Four Questions for Examining a Claim

First ask what the words mean. If happiness alternately means pleasure, satisfaction, achievement, security, and freedom without explanation, the argument loses continuity. We need not reduce them to one definition, but must say which is at issue.

Next ask why we should believe it. Is the basis personal experience, another's report, a survey, or mathematical proof? Each has strengths and limits. One person's experience is important evidence about what happened to them, not proof that the same happens throughout humanity.

Then ask whether another explanation is possible. Higher achievement among people using a learning method may not be caused solely by the method. Already motivated learners may choose it, or time, teachers, and environments may differ.

Finally ask what would change our minds. If any possible result counts as confirming our theory, those results at least do not test it. We may hold it as a philosophical position; calling it experimentally supported requires more.

These questions are not merely tools for defeating opponents. They make our own important ideas available for examination by others.

<a id="s4"></a>

## Valid Reasoning Can Begin from False Premises

“Everyone has the same wish. If everyone has the same wish, wishes do not conflict. Therefore, wishes do not conflict.” Even with an orderly inferential form, the first premise does not fit reality. Moreover, identical wishes can conflict when everyone wants the same single seat. Form and the content of premises must be examined separately.

Proof checks the route to a conclusion under specified premises and rules. Proof assistants can sometimes check that route rigorously. They do not automatically guarantee that real well-being is represented by those variables, or that affected people accept the premises.

This book's arguments share that limitation. We cannot construct an all-purpose chain that extracts truth from anywhere. Instead, we can make a chain whose empirical grounds, value commitments, and conditions can be traced.

## Questions That Become Scientific

Some philosophical questions move toward empirical research as definitions and measurements develop. From the broad question of mind, we can extract particular questions about attention, memory, and perception. Present unobservability does not make thought pointless.

Yet adding numbers does not itself make science. Rating “depth of consciousness” from one to ten only labels a mystery with numbers if what is measured remains unclear. We must examine stability, relationships with other methods, and discrimination between explanations.

Expanding science means neither declaring the unknown known nor merely changing its label. It means dividing a problem previously discussed in broad terms into smaller questions and specifying what to observe. Chapters 15–20 give concrete examples.
