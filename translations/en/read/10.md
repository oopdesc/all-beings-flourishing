# Chapter 10 Connecting AI with Shared Purposes

<a id="s2"></a>

## Capability Is Not Goodness

Outperforming humans on a task does not by itself authorize AI to determine life's purposes or social values. Computational and predictive abilities, experience, and responsibility are different questions.

This book treats AI as technology supporting human learning, thought, and action. Its capabilities need not be underestimated. Fluent expert answers, however, guarantee neither truth nor consciousness.

The Transformer paper presents an attention-centered computational architecture and task performance.[16] The technical term “attention” does not establish human-like subjective attention or self-consciousness. Functional names and experiential properties must not be confused.

<a id="s3"></a>

## Checking Explanations That Look Correct

AI can produce nonexistent references and incorrect explanations plausibly. NIST's generative AI risk guidance includes such erroneous generation.[11] Good writing and evidential support require separate checks.

For learning, go beyond requesting answers: ask what kind of support they have, consult originals, and test examples under different conditions. Check symbols and assumptions in equations. For code, distinguish running successfully from serving its purpose.

Repeatedly asking AI to check itself is not necessarily independent verification. It may repeat the same error or adapt explanations to the question. Combine primary materials, actual measurements, and different methods.

<a id="s4"></a>

## Even Recent Evaluations Have Lags and Limits

The February 2026 International AI Safety Report synthesizes capability improvements, uneven task performance, misinformation, and gaps between evaluation and use.[44] Its publication year does not mean it includes every development in that year.

The same applies here. High scores on difficult tests are evidence about those tasks. They do not jointly guarantee accurate citation checking, safe action in unfamiliar situations, or correct choices of social values.

Precisely because I hope to extend intelligence, I want to specify what to delegate and where humans verify. Evaluating outputs while hiding verification work can make answers seem more certain than they are. Expectations of capability should be separated from relinquishing responsibility.

<a id="s5"></a>

## Learning Purposes

Humans may not completely specify a purpose for AI. Emphasizing speed may omit safety and explanation; maximizing satisfaction may encourage hiding difficult problems.

Cooperative inverse reinforcement learning formulates human–machine cooperation where the machine initially lacks complete knowledge of human reward. Teaching, learning, and communication have roles.[26] In this formulation, the human knows the reward function and human and machine share the same reward. It does not solve conflicts among multiple humans' values.

Inverse reward design likewise treats a designed reward as information about an objective, rather than the complete final objective itself.[27] These studies do not settle value questions, but help resist treating objective descriptions as absolute.

Nor is our shared purpose a finished specification that can be handed to AI in one sentence. “Make everyone happy” omits the population, meaning of happiness, conflict handling, prohibited means, and authority to revise decisions. Machine intelligence alone must not fill every ambiguity; human deliberation and responsibility remain necessary.

<a id="s6"></a>

## Whose Purposes Are Learned

Preferences inferred from behavior need not reflect informed, free choice. Fear, habit, or environmental constraints can produce unwanted actions. Equating observed conduct with genuine wishes is dangerous.

There is also more than one human. Owners, users, workers, and affected outsiders have different interests. A system learning only the highest payer's wishes does not represent society merely by calling them shared purposes.

UNESCO's Recommendation on AI Ethics emphasizes human rights, dignity, and human oversight.[22] These directions need concrete conditions: who can stop a system, where errors can be reported, and who responds to harm.

<a id="s7"></a>

## Do Not Erase Disagreement as Noise

Two people may rate the same response differently because one misunderstands facts or because they value different things. Treating both as measurement error leaves one person's values as the answer. A 2026 social-choice survey describes value judgments in aggregating AI evaluations.[52] Evaluator selection, minority views, and actual post-training responses all require examination.

Some research compares guarantees for average utility in mathematical models with assumptions. Following severe worst-case examples in a 2025 study, a September 2026 preprint reexamines guarantees by separating distribution mismatch between comparison data and the reference constraining learning, and the treatment of rewards.[53][54] The former is not thereby meaningless, nor does the latter solve real value conflicts. Progress concerns what follows under which assumptions.

“Utility” inside a model is not a measurement of human well-being itself. Improving an average also does not determine how to treat severe minority harm. Implementing shared purposes requires distinguishing mathematically guaranteed properties from questions society must separately judge.

<a id="s8"></a>

## Getting Answers and Learning

Completing a task with AI and being able to solve the next task oneself are different outcomes. In a 2026 programming-learning preprint, 52 people learned a new library in a short experiment; the AI-access group scored lower on an unsupported comprehension test.[49] One task over a short period cannot establish conclusions about all AI or long-term intellectual ability.

In a high-school mathematics experiment, AI helped during practice but disadvantaged later unsupported performance; a learning-oriented design mitigated that disadvantage.[50] A university physics study found higher immediate test performance with carefully designed AI materials than classroom active learning.[51] Different populations, materials, and measurement periods prevent reducing these findings to one winner.

I therefore propose deciding what should remain in one's own understanding, beyond whether AI is used. Close the explanation and reconstruct its reasons. Solve a problem with one condition changed. Identify what is wrong in an incorrect answer. This is the book's practical proposal, not a procedure whose overall effectiveness those three studies proved. Use it as a starting point for checking whether understanding develops.

<a id="s9"></a>

## Greater Delegation Requires Verification

Delegation should depend on failure's consequences. Reversible practice exercises and decisions affecting others' lives or rights require different procedures. In consequential settings, constrain authority, keep records, and make human understanding and review possible.

I do not want AI to become a new authority to obey. I want it to be a partner or tool that supplements human limits while allowing humans to keep questioning. This requires mechanisms for discovering errors and learning without entrenched dependence, not speed alone.
