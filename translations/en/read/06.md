# Chapter 6 Rethinking the Boundary Between Self and Others

<a id="s2"></a>

## Why Our Own Experience Seems Closer

We know our hunger or anxiety without explanation; we learn others' through expressions, words, and actions. This asymmetry makes our interests feel urgent and theirs abstract.

Yet distance from me is not distance from them. Their suffering is central to their experience. Treating someone as background does not make their inner world background.

Questioning the boundary between self and others matters here. Recognizing that my perspective is not the entire world does not make me worthless. It acknowledges that others have perspectives too.

I do not wish to erase every difference between self and others. I want to acknowledge differences without letting proximity alone determine radically different worth. Special concern for family and friends is possible without counting distant suffering as zero from the outset.

<a id="s3"></a>

## Is All Subjectivity One

One idea within my reflections is that the subjects we call ourselves might fundamentally be the same subject, undergoing different experiences in different lives.

This is not a fact established by current science.

Related concerns appear in the open individualism discussed by Kolak.[34] My idea cannot be presented as unprecedented, and philosophical precedent is no substitute for scientific proof. Common features of brains or behavior differ from numerical identity of experiencing subjects. Two similarly structured machines are not thereby one machine. Moving from similarity to identity requires a further argument.

If a theory of one subject and a theory of separate subjects predict no different observations, current observation cannot choose between them. They may pose meaningful philosophical questions without being experimentally supported theories.

With that distinction, the idea may still have an ethical use: how would I want to be treated if I experienced the world from the other's position? Its usefulness, however, does not prove its cosmological truth.

<a id="s4"></a>

## Concern Does Not Require Identity

Must we prove that another is really ourselves before valuing them? I do not think so.

If subjects are separate, others have distinctive lives I cannot experience for them—a reason for respect. If something fundamental is shared, different bodies, memories, wishes, and relationships still exist. Neither possibility licenses mistreatment.

Given the fairness and concern adopted in Chapter 1, either hypothesis can support listening, seeking consent, and avoiding harm. Everyday concern should not depend wholly on an unanswered metaphysical question.

“You are me, so you should obey my wishes” contradicts this book. Treating another as an extension of oneself to dominate them makes a philosophy questioning boundaries erase the other instead.

<a id="s5"></a>

## Learning from Nonself

Buddhist traditions include questioning a fixed, independent self and ethical concern with suffering and its relief. They are not uniform, and nonself cannot simply be replaced by the claim that everyone is one consciousness.[19]

What I wish to learn is an unwillingness to freeze the self into an unchanging owner. Body, memory, language, and relationships make me who I am. Maintaining oneself need not conflict with accepting change.

Loosening attachment also differs from remaining silent under harm. If a philosophy questioning self-attachment is used to demand submission, that use must itself be questioned.

Looking at oneself from slightly farther away and others from slightly closer, we can change relationships without possessing a final theory of subjectivity. Humility about boundaries does not permit violating them.
