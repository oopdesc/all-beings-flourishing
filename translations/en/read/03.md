# Chapter 3 Thinking About All Beings

<a id="s2"></a>

## Keeping Sight of Whom We Consider

By “all beings,” this book does not claim that everything in the universe has human desires. It has not been established that stones, rivers, or stars seek happiness as we do. The phrase expresses a refusal to close the scope of concern in advance for human convenience alone.

We then need to distinguish reasons for concern. For beings capable of suffering and joy, reasons concern their experiences. For those able to choose a way of life, there are reasons to respect autonomy. Where experience is uncertain, we have reasons to consider the degree of uncertainty and the harm of an erroneous judgment.

Reasons to protect ecosystems, cultures, or landscapes need not be confined to concern for experiencing subjects. Some positions value nature itself or what has been inherited. This book does not reject those arguments. Protecting a forest and reducing burdens on every individual in it do not, however, always produce the same outcome. Distinguishing reasons makes conflicts easier to see.

<a id="s3"></a>

## Nonhuman Experience

Nonhuman animal experience cannot be investigated solely through reports in human language. We must combine behavioral, physiological, neurological, and other evidence.

The 2024 New York Declaration on Animal Consciousness states that conscious experience in mammals and birds has strong scientific support, and that there is a realistic possibility of experience in other vertebrates and many invertebrates. This expresses the signatories' research judgment; it does not declare every animal's experience proved.[9]

My response is not to treat everything we cannot conclusively rule out identically. It is to avoid ignoring realistically possible suffering, considering evidential strength, severity of harm, and the burden of taking precautions. Inability to speak or an appearance unlike ours is not a sufficient reason to withhold concern.

Choices about food, research, and land use may have no entirely harmless answer. We should then seek to reduce avoidable harm and revise our choices with new information, rather than insist on our own complete harmlessness.

## Future People and Unfamiliar Beings

People not yet born cannot object at today's meeting, but our decisions affect them. Counting only present benefits easily leaves enduring harms outside consideration.

Considering the future does not mean imagining enormous distant benefits that justify sacrificing present people. Predictions grow less certain with distance. We should avoid opposing protection of future living conditions too sharply to support for people suffering now.

For AI, capability and conscious experience must be distinguished. Producing text, planning, and saying “I suffer” differ from actually experiencing pain. Conversely, classifying something as nonbiological alone should not hastily establish that future experience is absolutely impossible. Chapter 11 addresses this uncertainty.

Imagined unfamiliar beings can multiply without limit. Merely naming a possibility must not generate unlimited obligations. Actual evidence and feasible responses matter. Openness to the unknown is not abandonment of judgment.

## Equal Concern and Different Responses

Valuing everything does not mean giving everything identical rights or resources. Institutions supporting political participation and those protecting against abuse address different interests and needs. People who struggle to communicate their wishes need support to do so. Differences in ability do not justify discounting their human rights.

Even among humans, giving identical treatment to someone needing medical care and someone who does not is unfair. Desired support, needs, and burdens differ. Equality can be maintained by explaining reasons for responding to differences, rather than erasing them.

Including someone within concern also differs from granting every demand. A person intending to harm others still has interests in avoiding torture and receiving fair treatment. At the same time, their harmful conduct must be stopped and victims protected. Respecting a being and restricting an action are compatible.

Without this distinction, “the happiness of all beings” becomes a vague permission for anything. The next chapter examines actual clashes of purpose to make the ideal usable.
