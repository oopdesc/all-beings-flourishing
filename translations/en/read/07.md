# Chapter 7 The Limits of Intelligence and Social Problems

<a id="s2"></a>

## Deciding Without Knowing Everything

We cannot learn everything about the world before acting. Choosing work or policy never involves complete foresight. Time to gather information, capacity for comparison, and attention are limited.

Simon's bounded rationality considers judgment under informational and computational constraints rather than as the work of an ideal calculator processing all information freely.[3] “Limits of intelligence” here does not disparage particular people. It concerns constraints on judging beings, myself included.

In complex societies, actions affect distant places and later times. Considering only visible benefits omits others' harm and deferred costs. Deeper understanding therefore matters ethically: good intentions may fail to prevent unseen harm.

More knowledge does not, however, necessarily make us better. Accurate prediction can support assistance or sophisticated exploitation. Intelligence can help fulfill purposes without automatically deciding whether those purposes are good.

<a id="s3"></a>

## Intelligence Alone Does Not Explain Social Problems

Explaining social problems solely as insufficient human intelligence obscures unequal benefits, concentrated power, responsibility, scarce resources, discrimination, and history.

Someone may knowingly continue pollution because institutions let them keep profits while imposing costs on others. Lack of understanding is not necessarily the cause. More explanatory materials alone will not change that incentive.

People with few options may choose conduct undesirable in the long term. Blaming intelligence or character does not expand their options. Support for judgment and support for living conditions must be considered together.

I also reject replacing good and bad with high and low intelligence. Intellectual abilities have many dimensions and do not determine dignity. Highly intelligent people can harm, and people judged less intelligent can care. Ranking humans on one scale and allocating a voice accordingly does not respect intelligence.

<a id="s4"></a>

## Making Individuals and Groups Wiser

A group of excellent experts can be biased if dissent is impossible. Conversely, people with limited individual knowledge can find possibilities unavailable to any one person by sharing information, correcting mistakes, and distributing responsibility.

Group size alone is not decisive. Whose information arrives and whose is ignored matters. If people with direct experience cannot attend, failure reports are punished, or only convenient numbers reach managers, existing information goes unused.

Improving social intelligence therefore includes education, clear explanations, records, verification, appeals, and ways to report internal problems. Increasing knowledge and opening routes for knowledge to travel are separate necessary tasks.

<a id="s5"></a>

## Finding Our Own Mistakes

The aspect of intelligence I particularly value is discovering and correcting errors: testing where an explanation fails, rather than merely using difficult words; specifying when confidence should change, rather than simply displaying confidence.

When making a proposal, ask why it might fail. Ask affected people what worries them. Before a trial, define success and stopping conditions. These are shareable procedures, not abilities reserved for geniuses.

AI can help, but merely multiplying plausible explanations can reinforce mistakes. Extending intelligence must extend verification, not just the supply of answers.

Human intelligence has limits; this does not diminish humanity's value. Those limits give us reasons to share knowledge and power and establish relationships of mutual correction. The next chapter examines the conditions for those relationships.
