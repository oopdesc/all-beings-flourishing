# Appendix A A Map of the Argument

The conclusions do not follow from one experiment or axiom. This map separates empirical findings, value commitments, conditional reasoning, and unverified hypotheses, showing where changes alter conclusions. Numbers aid rereading; they are not formal-verification certificates.

<a id="s2"></a>

## Starting Experiences and Values

**E1 The urgency of experience.** People experience and report pain, joy, and anxiety as important to them. Particular causes and degrees need separate examination. See Chapter 1.

**E2 Constraints on judgment.** Information, time, and computation limit human judgment. Abilities and outcomes differ; greater intelligence alone does not determine good purposes. See Chapters 7 and 10.[3][11]

**E3 Life has multiple dimensions.** Well-being and good lives include aspects not exhausted by one subjective score. Distinguish what is measured from what is valued. See Chapter 5.[1][24][38]

**V1 Do not exclude.** Do not dismiss others' interests merely because they are distant, differently able, or less powerful. This is the book's value commitment.

**V2 Avoid severe harm.** Reduce avoidable severe suffering and protect life and autonomy. Absence of suffering is not the only value.

**V3 Protect examination.** Preserve affected people's routes to understand, participate in, and object to setting or revising purposes.

E1–E3 alone cannot compel agreement with V1–V3. Moving from empirical description to value judgment requires premises about what matters.

<a id="s3"></a>

## Conditional Implications

**I1 Do not disregard others' harm without reason.** Given V1 and V2, changing treatment of comparable harm solely because the affected person's name changes is inconsistent. Different treatment needs relevant reasons. Chapters 1, 3, and 6 explain this.

**I2 Do not specify unconditional fulfillment of every wish.** Incompatible wishes and wishes to harm others prevent that specification. Reconsider purposes, means, and distribution while preserving V2 and V3. See Chapter 4.

**I3 Design cooperation's conditions.** Given E2 and conflicts between individual and collective interests, understanding alone leaves problems. Examine burdens, benefits, responsibility, information, and review. See Chapters 8, 9, and 15.[4][5][43]

**I4 Revise purposes too.** Given E2 and V3, current purposes and decision makers should not become incorrigible authorities. Explain reasons and responsibility for revision and preserve examination. See Chapters 10, 14, and 21.

**I5 Check before and after action.** Given E2 and E3 alongside V1 and V2, intentions and averages alone cannot establish success. Examine differing effects and disadvantages. See Chapters 5, 16, 17, and 22.

This supports a provisional shared purpose: reduce exclusion from concern, avoid severe harm, and cooperate to expand conditions for choosing one's life anew. It includes value premises and empirical conditions, not a uniquely derived cosmic command.

<a id="s4"></a>

## Hypotheses Not Required for the Conclusion

**H1 Fundamental identity of subjects.** This book does not scientifically establish that all subjectivity belongs to one subject. V1 and V2 can support concern without it. See Chapter 6.[34]

**H2 Cosmic self-transcendence.** Biological and cultural change do not entail one cosmic will or inevitable movement toward happiness. See Chapters 14 and 19.

**H3 Artificial experience.** Capability and self-reports alone do not establish subjective suffering or joy. Theory, internal mechanisms, and calibrated evaluation remain issues. See Chapters 11 and 18.[36][39][40][41]

**H4 Ultimate truth and purpose.** Whether existence has an ultimate purpose, and whether that question is well formed, remain open. This does not make every speculation equally likely. See Chapters 19 and 24.

Changing one hypothesis does not erase every reason for everyday concern and research. Keeping grand hypotheses partly independent of practical reasons makes the book easier to revise.
