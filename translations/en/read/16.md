# Chapter 16 Fair Judgment Cannot Be Decided by Numbers Alone

<a id="s2"></a>

## Is the Largest Total Enough

Consider three options affecting three people. Their scores are A: 10, 10, −2; B: 5, 5, 5; C: 4, 7, 6. These fictional explanatory values assume actual well-being could be measured on one common scale.

Totals are 18, 15, and 17, so maximizing the total selects A. But if the third person's −2 includes a severe rights violation, the total alone cannot make it acceptable.

One method first excludes options violating minimum protections. If all scores must be at least zero in this example, A is excluded and B and C remain.

Zero is not an ethics discovered in nature; it is a rule stipulated for the example. Real thresholds require law, rights, affected people's views, and the nature of harm. Numbers must not hide that judgment.

<a id="s3"></a>

## The Worst-Off Person or the Total

Among B and C, the larger total selects C; maximizing the lowest score selects B. Calculation alone does not choose the criterion.

Moving from B to C lowers the first score from five to four while raising the second to seven and third to six. It is not a change that improves someone without worsening anyone. It involves both benefits and burdens.

Parfit distinguishes concern about inequality itself from giving greater weight to benefits for the worse off.[32] “For everyone” should not erase that difference.

Looking only at the minimum can miss large gains elsewhere; looking at totals can miss severe minority burdens. I therefore offer no universal ethical equation. Explain the criterion and what it leaves unseen.

<a id="s4"></a>

## What Can and Cannot Be Compared

A change leaving everyone at least as well off and someone better off is generally called a Pareto improvement. If available, there is strong reason to consider it.

Still, check what improvement means, effects on outsiders, and future burdens. Improving outcomes for the three visible people by harming someone else is not an overall improvement.

Absence of remaining Pareto improvements also does not establish fairness. Even a highly unequal distribution may be impossible to improve without worsening someone. Efficiency and fairness are separate properties.

Determine what can be compared and treat remaining choices as value judgments involving affected people. Showing where comparison is unsettled advances discussion more than hiding it.

<a id="s5"></a>

## Predictions Are Uncertain Too

In reality, option scores themselves are unknown. Predictions include error and overlooked effects. Do not choose one convenient estimate and declare an optimum.

If predicted effects range from +1 to +3 for one person and −1 to +2 for another, improvement for everyone is not guaranteed. The second may be harmed. Further inquiry or a small trial may reduce useful uncertainty.

Uncertainty concerns values as well as predictions. Research on moral uncertainty proposes considering multiple ethical theories.[28] Other work criticizes placing different theories on one common scale.[29] This book does not treat the debate as settled by one formula.

<a id="s6"></a>

## Deciding While Preserving Correction

Uncertainty cannot justify never deciding: inaction has consequences too. Choose using current evidence and prepare a response to discovered errors at the same time.

Who checks effects? When does harm require stopping? Where can affected people complain? Who explains unmet promises? These are part of judgment, not additions after calculation.

Considering all beings' happiness does not mean becoming a single calculator with all information. It means creating routes through which people omitted from our calculation can enter again.
